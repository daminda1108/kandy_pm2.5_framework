## Previous measurements in Kandy {#s-two-decades-measurement}

{{tbl:T3_1}}

The studies in {{tbl:T3_1}} fall into three types: records at a single point, short campaigns,
and national surfaces trained largely on data from elsewhere. None provides the spatially and
temporally continuous field that the problems described in {{ref:ch-kandy-setting-record-stakes}}
require.

The earliest study bears directly on the seasonal behaviour of the field. Abeyratne and
Ileperuma measured sulphur dioxide, nitrogen dioxide and ozone at fixed sites and grouped the
results by monsoon [@Abeyratne2006], finding the maximum in the north-east monsoon rather than
the south-west. Because Sri Lanka's main industrial and urban sources lie to the south-west of
Kandy, a domestic-source explanation would predict the opposite ordering, and the result is
consistent with long-range transport during the north-east monsoon. The measurement predates this
study and concerns different pollutants. The field reconstructed here shows the same ordering,
with its seasonal maximum in the north-east monsoon, so the earlier result provides independent
corroboration of that behaviour.

The roadside survey of Elangasinghe and Shanthini is the principal evidence on spatial
variability within the city. Twenty-five sites in and around the city, from the bus terminus to
rural control sites, were each sampled for PM10 over the same three midday hours, on different
days, with traffic counts recorded alongside. From the roadside at the entrance to the Peradeniya
botanical gardens to a point inside the gardens about three hundred metres from the road, the
concentration fell from 110 to about 4 micrograms per cubic metre, and over the twenty roadside
sites whose traffic was counted the correlation with traffic intensity reached an R-squared of
0.82 [@Elangasinghe2008]. The observed spread across the whole survey is about
{{claim:spatial.obs_spread}} times, although the sites were sampled on different days.

The source apportionment of Seneviratne and colleagues, using positive matrix factorisation of
speciated samples at Katugastota, attributes 7.6 per cent of PM2.5 mass to traffic and 14.1 per
cent to biomass burning [@Seneviratne2017]. This contradicts, at least for that suburban site,
the assumption made in earlier versions of this work that Kandy's particulate matter is
approximately ninety per cent vehicular; {{ref:s-defects-found-audit-rather}} describes how that
assumption persisted and what it affected.

More recent studies provide the only independent data against which the model can be evaluated
at Kandy. Nirmani and colleagues published daily concentrations obtained from the National
Building Research Organisation for 2021 and 2022, at 360 days in each year [@Nirmani2025].
Dhammapala analysed low-cost sensors across Sri Lanka, including one at Akurana near Kandy,
against the reference monitor in Colombo [@Dhammapala2022]. Attanayake and colleagues built a
machine-learned surface for Sri Lanka from 24 low-cost sensors calibrated against reference
monitors in Colombo and at Torrington Park, Kandy [@Attanayake2025].
{{ref:s-checks-kandy-carry-weight}} compares the model with all three.
