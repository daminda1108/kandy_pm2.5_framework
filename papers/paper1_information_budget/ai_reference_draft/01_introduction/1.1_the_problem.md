# 1 Introduction

## 1.1 The problem: cities that must decide what to measure

Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is the environmental exposure with the largest measured burden of
disease, and the World Health Organization lowered its 24-hour guideline to 15 µg m⁻³ in 2021
[@WHO2021]. Acting on that guideline requires knowing, day by day, how polluted a city is. Many
cities cannot: they have no regulatory monitor at all, or one instrument for a whole urban area.

The gap is not evenly spread. Of the 20,179 PM2.5 locations in the OpenAQ archive [@OpenAQ], we
found 6 deep-tropical city clusters (within 15° of the equator) with at least ten concurrent reference
monitors, against 65 temperate ones (Section 2.1.3). The places with the least monitoring are, as a
rule, the places with the least money to build it, and where a first investment has to be chosen
carefully.

Global estimates exist for every city. Satellite products combine aerosol optical depth, chemical
transport models and land use to map surface PM2.5 at about 1 km [@vanDonkelaar2021; @Wei2023],
and reanalyses describe the meteorology that drives its daily variation [@Hersbach2020]. These
products are, however, calibrated against ground monitors, so their skill is highest where monitors
already are and their errors are least known where monitors are absent. A city without monitors
cannot simply adopt them; it has to decide what to measure, and in what order, to make a local
estimate trustworthy.

That decision has several parts. Should the first money buy a local sensor, or access to a regional
background series? Is a second sensor worth much once there is one? Does the answer depend on what
the estimate is for: the ordinary day, or the day the guideline is crossed? And how much of what a
city could learn from free global data is it already using before it buys anything? These are
questions about the **value of information** [@Howard1966], asked for a city rather than for a
single decision.
