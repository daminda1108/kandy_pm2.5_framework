## 1.2 What is known, and what is not

**Network design.** A long literature asks where to place monitors within one region, by statistical,
evolutionary or multi-objective optimisation; Verghese and Nema review 77 such studies
[@Verghese2022]. The closest recent work couples an advection–diffusion model with a Kalman
smoother and a decision-theoretic value of information to place 10 to 40 low-cost sensors in one
coastal region of Texas [@Choi2026]. Its accuracy differed between placement strategies by only
about 1.5 to 2 per cent, which agrees with our own finding that deliberate siting does not measurably
beat convenience siting (Section 2.8). Bayesian optimal design gives the formal basis for choosing
observations to reduce uncertainty [@Chaloner1995].

**What these studies do not ask.** They optimise *where* to put instruments of one kind, inside one
region, usually against a model's own notion of uncertainty. They do not measure, across many cities,
what each *type* of observation is worth: a first local sensor, a denser network, a background series,
a satellite retrieval or a map of the city's surface, each against the estimate a city could already
build without it.

**Low-cost sensors** have made the first local measurement affordable [@Morawska2018], which makes
the question practical: a city can buy one or two instruments long before it can buy a regulatory
network, and needs to know what they will add.

**The satellite products' calibration is circular.** Products trained on ground monitors carry
information from those monitors [@vanDonkelaar2021; @Wei2023]. Used as a covariate, such a
product already holds part of what a monitor would add, and can therefore change how a local
monitor compares with other observations (Section 3.4.2).

**The statistical trap.** Cities differ several-fold in how well they can be estimated at all. A
comparison of two observation streams made by taking each stream's median gain across cities can
therefore point the opposite way from the comparison made within each city, an instance of Simpson's
paradox [@Simpson1951]. We met this reversal repeatedly in our own analyses before making
within-city pairing a rule (Section 2.5).

The gap is therefore specific: nobody has measured the marginal value of observation-stream types
across a global panel of cities, under declared information budgets, with paired inference and
pre-registered tests.
