## 1.3 Approach

We simulate, in cities that are densely monitored, the position of a city that is not. In each city
some stations are hidden and used only for scoring; the rest are released one budget at a time. An
**information budget** is a declared list of the data streams an estimate may use, and a **ladder** of
nested budgets adds one stream at each rung: global reanalysis drivers, the city's static geography, a
daily satellite retrieval, the first two local stations, stations three to six, and a background
series. The gain of each rung is the reduction in error it buys over the rung below. Declaring the
budgets and checking in code that each rung uses exactly its budget (Section 2.4) is what makes a gain
attributable to the stream that produced it.

Three design choices carry the paper. Every comparison between streams is **paired within city** and
intervals come from a **two-level bootstrap** over networks and cities (Section 2.5). Every effect is
averaged over **many random splits** of a city's stations, so that no result rests on one draw
(Section 2.3.5). And the study is split: an estimator developed and checked on **47 discovery cities**
in 28 countries was frozen and then tested **once** on **76 cities never used**, against endpoints
registered before their data were retrieved (Section 2.6).

## 1.4 Contributions

1. A registered measurement, confirmed on 72 fresh cities, of what each type of observation adds to a
   sensorless daily estimate of city PM2.5: the first two local stations reduce error by about 9 %
   (less than half the discovery estimate), four more add a quarter of a point, and a same-network
   background series adds about 40 %.
2. A demonstration that the registered **ranking** of a background series above the first local stations
   comes from how the rungs use their stations, not from what the stations measure: used on the day, two
   stations of any kind reduce daily error by close to 58 %, and the background's lead disappears
   (Section 3.1.8; post hoc). The value of a station lies in its daily reading.
3. A demonstration that a covariate trained on monitors **tilts** the registered ranking toward the
   background rung (Section 3.4.2; exploratory), rather than inflating every rung as is usually feared.
4. Registered evidence that the registered gains are **not artefacts of the starting estimate, the
   learner or the number of stations**: they survive a richer sensorless baseline built from five more free streams,
   three alternative learners including deep learning, none of which beats gradient boosting, and each
   city's full station network (Sections 3.1.5–3.1.7).
5. A set of **bounded nulls** for fine-scale spatial structure: no land-use predictor, learned pattern,
   Earth-observation embedding or model family beats a single built-up-area covariate by more than each
   test could detect (Section 2.8).
6. A registered measurement of what additional stations buy for a within-city map (Section 3.6), with a
   post-hoc re-analysis of its noise: at three to eight stations neither interpolation nor any free
   product, including a satellite PM2.5 surface, ranks neighbourhoods usefully (rank correlation about
   0.1); after correction for multiple testing interpolation clearly overtakes a free layer in 3 of 23
   cities; siting by design gains nothing; tropical cities cannot be distinguished from temperate ones.
7. A methods contribution: declared, admissibility-checked budgets; paired inference; split averaging;
   and a discovery/confirmation design, with every defect found along the way recorded and corrected
   (Sections 2.10–2.11), including one the design could not catch: a registered comparison between arms
   built differently (Section 4.3).
