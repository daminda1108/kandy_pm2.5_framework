## 2.2 Simulating a city without monitors, and scoring it

Each city in the panel plays two roles at once. Most of its stations are hidden from the model and
used only to score it; a few are released one rung at a time, as the observations a city might
acquire. The other 47 cities supply everything that can be learned without local measurements.

### 2.2.1 Held-out stations and the scoring target

For each city *c*, the station identifiers are shuffled with a fixed seed and the first
$n_H = \max(3, \lfloor n_c/3 \rfloor)$ stations form the held-out set $\mathcal{H}_c$ (3 to 16
stations, median 4). A station-day counts only if at least 18 of its 24 hours are valid, the
completeness rule used for PM2.5 in US regulatory practice [@CFR50N]; its value
is that day's mean. The scoring target on day *t* is the equal-weight mean over the held-out stations
with a valid day,

$$y_{c,t} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{c,t}|}\sum_{s \in \mathcal{H}_{c,t}} y_{s,t},$$

evaluated on the days when at least one held-out station has a valid day. (Without the rule, a
station reporting two hours counted as much as one reporting twenty-four; the rule matters most in
the deep tropics, where many low-cost stations report partial days [F.115].) A city is scored only if it has at
least 120 such days. The remaining stations form a pool $\mathcal{P}_c$, in random order, from
which the observation rungs draw (Section 2.3).

Holding stations out, rather than holding out time, answers the question a city without monitors
faces: how close would an estimate built from whatever it can acquire be to what dense
monitoring would have measured, over the same days.

### 2.2.2 Skill and gain

For rung *j*, skill is the root-mean-square error over the city's held-out days,

$$R_{c,j} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T_c}\sum_{t=1}^{T_c}\big(\tilde{y}^{(j)}_{c,t} - y_{c,t}\big)^2},$$

where $\tilde{y}^{(j)}_{c,t}$ is the rung's prediction after shrinkage (Section 2.3.4). The value of
the observation added at rung *j* is its **gain**, the percentage reduction in error relative to
the rung below:

$$G_{c,j} = 100\,\frac{R_{c,j-1} - R_{c,j}}{R_{c,j-1}}.$$

The gain is computed for each city on each of 21 station splits, and the city's gain is the median
over splits (Section 2.3.5). Gains are summarised across cities by the median of per-city gains,
never by the gain of median errors. The distinction matters: cities differ several-fold in their
baseline error, and a ratio of medians is dominated by which cities happen to be large or small
(Section 2.5).

### 2.2.3 What the ladder measures, and what it does not

The target is the **daily city-mean** concentration. The ladder therefore values each observation
for estimating how polluted the city is on each day, not where within the city pollution is
highest; the second question is the subject of Sections 2.7 and 2.8. Because the target averages
over held-out stations, part of the value of local observations comes from matching the level and
variability of the city's own network, a point we return to in Section 4.1.
