## 2.3 The information-budget ladder

### 2.3.1 Declared budgets

An information budget is a declared list of the data streams an estimate may use. The ladder is a
sequence of nested budgets, each adding one stream to the one before it:

| rung | budget | stream added |
|---|---|---|
| Bud0a | reanalysis drivers | what any city can compute from global reanalysis |
| Bud0b | + static geography | the city's built and natural surface |
| Bud0c | + satellite level | a daily satellite retrieval (MAIAC AOD) |
| Bud1 | + two local stations | the first local observations |
| Bud2 | + stations three to six | a denser local network |
| Bud3 | + a background series | a low-percentile series from the city's other stations |

`Bud0c` is the sensorless rung: everything a city can assemble without a single monitor. Declaring
the budgets in advance, and checking in code that each rung uses exactly its budget (Section 2.4),
is what makes the difference between two rungs attributable to the stream that separates them.

### 2.3.2 The sensorless rung

The sensorless rungs are fitted by a histogram-based gradient-boosting regressor (learning rate
0.06, at most 300 iterations, other settings at their defaults [scikit-learn 1.8.0, @Pedregosa2011]) trained
leave-one-city-out:

$$\hat{y}^{(0)}_{c,t} = \operatorname{median}_{k=1}^{5} f^{(k)}_{-c}(\mathbf{x}_{c,t}),$$

where $f^{(k)}_{-c}$ is trained on every city except *c* with learner seed *k*, and $\mathbf{x}_{c,t}$
holds the features in the rung's budget. A city contributes no PM2.5 observation to its own
sensorless prediction; the code asserts this for every fitted model. With more than 10,000 training
rows the learner holds out a random 10% of training rows for early stopping, so a single seed is one
draw of the fitted model; taking the median over five seeds removes that arbitrary variance, which
alone moved the first-station gain by 12 points in a single-seed analysis [F.115]. Missing satellite
days are passed to the learner as missing values, which it handles natively.

### 2.3.3 The observation rungs

Each city's stations are shuffled by a seed; the first $n_H$ form the held-out scoring set
(Section 2.2) and the rest a pool $\mathcal{P}_c$ in shuffled order. `Bud1` uses the first two pool
stations, `Bud2` the first six, so the step from `Bud1` to `Bud2` adds stations three to six. The
stations are placed where the network happened to put them, not by any siting design; deliberate
siting is tested separately (Section 2.8).

A local station enters as a recalibration of the sensorless estimate. For rung $j \in \{1, 2\}$, the
daily mean of the rung's stations is regressed on the sensorless prediction by ordinary least
squares,

$$\bar{y}_{\mathcal{S}_j,t} = a_{c,j} + b_{c,j}\,\hat{y}^{(0)}_{c,t} + \varepsilon_t,
\qquad \hat{y}^{(j)}_{c,t} = a_{c,j} + b_{c,j}\,\hat{y}^{(0)}_{c,t},$$

so a local station corrects the level and scale of the sensorless estimate; its reading on a given
day does not enter that day's prediction.

The background rung adds a **same-network background series**, the daily 10th percentile of the
city's remaining pool stations, $b_{c,t} = Q_{0.1}\{y_{s,t} : s \in \mathcal{P}_c \setminus \mathcal{S}_2\}$,
through a second regression,

$$\bar{y}_{\mathcal{S}_2,t} = \alpha + \beta\,\hat{y}^{(0)}_{c,t} + \gamma\, b_{c,t} + \varepsilon_t .$$

It is not a regional or rural monitor: the stations are few and from the same network, chosen with
no distance criterion. A background computed the same way from a different city's network 30 to
300 km away recovers a median of 71% [44, 82] of this rung's gain per city (Section 3.x) [F.115].
Because its coefficient is estimated against local stations, a background cannot be valued before
any local station exists.

**Two uses, reported separately.** In the *reconstruction* arm the coefficients are fitted over the
period that is scored: the use of a city estimating its past concentrations with sensors that ran
over the same period. In the *prospective* arm the coefficients, and the shrinkage weights below,
are fitted on the earlier half of each city's record and the later half is scored: the use of a city
that calibrates and then relies on the model. The two answer different questions and are named in
every result.

### 2.3.4 Shrinkage, chosen without the scoring stations

Each rung is combined with the rung below by a shrinkage weight,

$$\tilde{y}^{(j)}_{c,t} = \tilde{y}^{(j-1)}_{c,t} + w_{j}\big(\hat{y}^{(j)}_{c,t} - \tilde{y}^{(j-1)}_{c,t}\big),
\qquad w_{j} \in [0, 1].$$

A city's own optimal weight could only be chosen against its held-out stations, the scoring target,
which a city without a dense network does not have. The weight applied to city *c* is therefore
**cross-fitted**: the median of the weights that minimise error in every *other* city, per rung
[F.116]. (Version 1 chose each city's weight against its own held-out stations; Section 3.x reports
both.) A rung can then be worse than its parent out of sample, so a gain near zero means no usable
improvement, and a negative gain is possible and reported.

### 2.3.5 One estimate per city: averaging over the random design

Which stations a city happens to hold out and receive is a random draw, and so is the learner's
fit. Each per-city effect is therefore the **median over 21 station splits** (one seed per split)
of that city's gain, computed with the five-seed sensorless rung. A single split had moved the
headline deep-tropical comparison from an interval excluding zero to one containing it in 17 of 20
alternative splits [F.115]; averaging removes this variance before any comparison across cities is
made (Section 2.5).
