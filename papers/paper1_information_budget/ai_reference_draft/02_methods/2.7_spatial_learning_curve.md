## 2.7 The spatial learning curve: how much does each additional monitor buy for the map?

The ladder of Sections 2.3 to 2.6 values observations for one target, the daily city-mean
concentration. A city also needs a map. A sensor that adds nothing to the city mean may still
tell us where within the city concentrations are high, and the two questions can have different
answers. We therefore registered a second, separate test before any data were scored: how does
the skill of predicting concentrations at unmonitored sites within a city change as the number
of monitors grows? [OSF rqn4y; amendments 26hp8, 4whsc and a third amendment, see Section 2.9]

### 2.7.1 Estimand

For a city with a set of reference monitoring sites, we hold out a set of sites, fit an
estimator on *k* of the remaining sites, and predict the held-out sites. Skill is the Spearman
rank correlation between predicted and observed concentrations at the held-out sites,

$$\rho_{c,e,k} = \operatorname{Spearman}\big(\hat{y}_{e}(\mathcal{H}_c \mid \mathcal{F}_{c,k}),\; y(\mathcal{H}_c)\big),$$

where *c* indexes the city, *e* the estimator, $\mathcal{F}_{c,k}$ the fitting set of size *k* and
$\mathcal{H}_c$ the held-out set. The rank correlation asks whether an estimator puts the
held-out sites in the right order, which is the property a map must have to direct attention to
the most polluted neighbourhoods. It is insensitive to a uniform offset, which the ladder already
measures. The learning curve is $\rho_{c,e,k}$ as a function of *k*. It is computed for a static
target (each site's mean over the analysis window, design Q1) and a daily target (each day's
site means, design Q2).

### 2.7.2 Data and city frame

Hourly PM2.5 from reference-grade monitors was taken from the OpenAQ archive [@OpenAQ];
low-cost sensors were excluded, because the test concerns what a dense regulatory network can
reveal. Candidate monitors were grouped into city clusters by greedy 25 km clustering. Stations
within 100 m of each other were merged into one site before any split, so that a co-located twin
could never place a site's own value in its fitting set. Timestamps were converted to UTC before
daily aggregation. Quality control removed values outside [0, 1000) µg m⁻³, runs of more than 24
identical hourly values, and sites whose annual mean fell outside [5, 150] µg m⁻³. For each city,
the analysis window was the 365-day period that maximised the number of sites present on at least
75% of days, where a day counted as present with at least 18 hourly values.

These rules, registered before the records were retrieved, froze three frames. The primary frame (cities with at
least 20 eligible sites) holds 18 cities with 745 sites in 7 countries: 13 temperate, 4
subtropical and 1 tropical. A secondary frame (15 to 19 sites) holds 15 cities with 258 sites,
and a band arm of tropical and deep-tropical cities with at least 12 sites holds 6 cities with 91
sites. The registered stop rule required at least 10 primary cities. A sensitivity frame at 70%
day coverage (S-1), declared before scoring, holds 19 primary cities with 784 sites and is reported
beside the registered result, never in its place. S-1 exists because at 75% Bangkok, the only
dense deep-tropical network, missed the band arm by one site.

The primary frame is dominated by temperate networks, and three of its four cities that are
enclosed by terrain are Korean. The frame therefore describes how skill grows where dense
reference networks exist, and the band arm is too small to establish whether the same curve holds
in the tropics. We state this in Section 4.4 rather than here, because it limits interpretation,
not the estimand.

### 2.7.3 Splits, orderings and replicates

For each city and replicate, a held-out set of $H = \max(10, \lfloor n/3 \rfloor)$ sites is drawn at
random and kept fixed while *k* grows, so every point on a curve is scored on the same sites. A
blocked variant, holding out the *H* sites nearest a random point, is reported beside it. The
remaining sites are ordered in one of three ways, each representing a way a city might add
monitors: at random (the primary ordering); by a conditioned Latin hypercube over the seven
covariates (a deliberate design that spans the covariate space); and by descending length of
major road within 300 m (a convenience design that mimics siting near traffic). The fitting set
at each *k* is the first *k* sites of the ordering, so random and convenience fitting sets are
nested. The Latin hypercube is an optimal subset for a given size and has no nested form, so it is
drawn independently at each *k* (deviation D-1).

The *k* grid is {3, 5, 8, 12, 18, 25, 35, 50, 70}, truncated at *n* − *H* for each city. Each
city receives 100 replicates. Random draws use a generator seeded by (20260911, city, replicate),
so any replicate can be reproduced alone.

### 2.7.4 Estimators

Twelve estimators span the approaches a city could use. Their registered definitions are:

| id | estimator | what it represents |
|---|---|---|
| E0 | the mean of the fitting sites | a city that assumes it is uniform (a floor: ρ ≡ 0) |
| E1 | built-up land cover within 2.4 km of each site | the best single free raster (Section 2.8) |
| E2 | ridge regression on seven covariates, standardised on fitting sites only | land-use regression |
| E3 | ordinary kriging with an exponential variogram fitted to the *k* sites | spatial interpolation |
| E4 | inverse-distance weighting, power 2 | simple interpolation |
| E5 | E2 plus ordinary kriging of E2's in-sample residuals | regression kriging |
| E6 | geographically weighted regression, adaptive bisquare kernel, AICc, *k* ≥ 25 | local regression |
| E7 | the grey-box spatial pattern, in the two cities where it exists | a physics-shaped prior |
| E8 | TabPFN regressor (package defaults) on the covariates and coordinates | a tabular foundation model |
| E9 | E8 with the kriging prediction as an extra feature, computed leave-one-out on the fitting sites | a hybrid |
| E10 | a convolutional Gaussian neural process (ConvGNP) | a deep spatial learner |
| E11 | a transformer neural process (TNP-D) | a deep spatial learner |

E9 uses a leave-one-out kriging feature because in-sample kriging reproduces each fitting site's
own value, which would place the target inside its own predictor. All scaling and variogram
parameters are fitted on the fitting sites alone, and no covariate is derived from PM2.5.
E8 and E9 are run on CPU only, because TabPFN's default precision setting uses mixed-precision
arithmetic on a GPU and full precision on a CPU, so that its package defaults are a different
numerical estimator on each device (deviation D-8, Section 2.9).

The two deep learners are trained on daily fields in five folds grouped by country, with early
stopping on training-fold cities only and three seeds whose median is reported. Before any real
data were scored, each had to pass a registered positive control: on unseen synthetic cities
drawn from a Gaussian random field, its skill at 35 context sites had to come within 0.05 of
oracle kriging. E10 passed, at 0.610, 0.599 and 0.604 on three seeds against an oracle of 0.635.
E11 failed, at −0.027, −0.104 and −0.006. We checked the implementation before accepting this:
the same code learns a random plane (ρ = 1.000) and a smooth field (ρ = 0.974). Under the
registered rule, E11's real-data results are not interpreted. [A2; control summaries]

### 2.7.5 Registered comparisons

Every effect is a paired difference within city, summarised as the median over cities, with a
95% interval from a two-level bootstrap that resamples countries and then cities within each
country (5,000 draws). A difference of medians is never reported as an effect (Section 2.5).
Pooled curve levels are reported with their city count at each *k*, because large *k* exists only
in large cities and the set of cities contributing to a pooled level changes along the curve.
Within-city increments do not have this problem.

Three quantities summarise a curve. The **saturation point** $k^{*}$ is the first *k* whose paired
increment over the previous *k* has an interval containing zero and a median below 0.02. The
**crossover** $k^{\times}$ is the smallest *k* at which an interpolating estimator's advantage over
the benchmark E1 excludes zero, computed per city by bootstrapping replicates. The **reach** is the
distance from the nearest fitting site beyond which kriging no longer beats the uniform city:
held-out errors are standardised by the fitting sites' spread, binned by distance (0, 0.5, 1, 2,
3, 5, 8 and 12 km), and the reach is the first bin whose bootstrap interval for the paired error
difference reaches zero. A within-cell ceiling, predicting each site from the other sites in its
1 km cell where at least eight exist, bounds how much any 1 km map can explain.

The registration states eight primary predictions (X1 to X8) and five for the deep learners (X9 to
X13), each with a decision rule fixed in advance; they are listed in Table S2 with their rules
and verdicts. Rules framed as the absence of an effect are judged against a minimum detectable
effect: the smallest paired difference that a one-sided Wilcoxon test (α = 0.05) detects with 80%
power, given a between-city standard deviation of 0.20, evaluated at the number of primary
countries (seven). No correction for multiplicity is applied to X1 to X13, and none is claimed.
The second amendment adds an exploratory analysis (X-T) of whether terrain moderates the curve, using
four terrain descriptors, four curve outcomes and sixteen uncorrected tests; it can detect only
rank correlations of about 0.5 or larger and is reported as exploratory.
X6's second clause, that the ranking of estimators agrees between the two designs, was not implemented
in the scoring code; the omission is declared in Section 2.10.2 and cannot change X6's verdict.

### 2.7.6 Checks run before scoring

Two gates had to pass before any real city was scored. A synthetic positive control, a 60-site
Gaussian random field with a 4 km length scale, checked that kriging recovers a known field:
E3 reached 0.540 against 0.554 for an oracle that knows the true covariance, at *k* = 35. A leakage
self-test checked that merging co-located instruments works: with a site's twin in the fitting
set, kriging must reproduce the site with a standardised error below 0.1. The first implementation
failed at 2.03 because it compared instruments that replaced each other in time rather than
running together; comparing each pair on the days both reported, the corrected test passed at
0.080, against 0.699 without the twin, over 24 concurrent pairs in 9 cities. The margin below 0.1
is modest. Both corrections were declared before scoring (deviation D-7).
Of the 47 merged pairs, 23 shared fewer than 30 days (5 none) and were skipped (the lodged
amendment says 22; Section 2.10.2, D-7).

### 2.7.8 Extension: the same curve on full station records

The registered curve drew each site's data from a single year (Section 2.7.2), so the freeze rule could not
find a better-covered year that a site's longer record might contain. A registered extension (OSF fu59b)
re-ran the design with each candidate location's full archive record and every other rule unchanged:
candidates, freeze rule and frames, predictors, estimators E0 to E7, replicates and inference. The deep
arms (E8 to E11) were not re-run, so X9 to X12 were not re-tested. Three endpoints were registered: the
frame itself, reported before any curve (F1); X1 to X7 under the registered rules (F2); and, if three or
more tropical or deep-tropical cities qualified, an exploratory tropical arm compared with the envelope of
the non-tropical curves at its own detection limit (F3). Run on the registered one-year records, the
re-pointed freeze reproduced the registered frames exactly, and the registered leakage self-test (0.029)
and positive control passed on the new frame. One deviation was declared before scoring (B-1,
Section 2.10.2).
