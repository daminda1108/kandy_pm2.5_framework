## 2.8 Bounded tests of whether a better model can substitute for monitors

The spatial learning curve (Section 2.7) asks what monitors buy for a map. A city without
monitors will ask the opposite question first: can a better model, fed only freely available
data, recover the within-city pattern that monitors would have measured? We tested this with a
set of experiments that share one frame, one estimand, one benchmark and one detection limit, so
that their negative results can be read as bounds rather than as absences.

### 2.8.1 Frame and estimand

The frame is every panel station with at least 500 observations, 636 stations in 47 cities, each
described by 60 static predictors drawn from global datasets: road length by class within 50 to
1000 m, distance to the nearest major road, vegetation index, tree cover, surface water,
land-cover fractions, built-up volume, population and night-time lights, each at buffers of 100,
300, 1000 and 2400 m [F.61; @Didan2021; @Hansen2013; @Pekel2016; @Schiavina2023; @Pesaresi2023; @Elvidge2017; @Zanaga2022; @OpenStreetMap]. The target for each station is the mean of its
record. Records are not concurrent across stations, which adds noise to each city's ranking but
cannot bias a comparison between two estimators scored on the same stations.

The estimand is the within-city Spearman correlation between predicted and observed station
means, computed for each city and summarised as the median across cities. Predictors and target
are standardised within each city, so no model can score by learning which cities are more
polluted than others; only the ranking of stations within a city counts.

### 2.8.2 Admissibility: what a city without monitors may use

A method is admissible only if it could run in a city with no monitors. We therefore use
leave-one-city-out validation: when a city is scored, none of its own PM2.5 observations enters
any fitted quantity, and coordinates and city identifiers are removed from the predictors.
Kriging, geographically weighted regression and inverse-distance weighting are inadmissible by
this rule, because each estimates a surface from measurements inside the target city
[F.105]. We nonetheless ran them as an oracle, with the target city's own stations visible, to
show what the inadmissible methods achieve when given data the monitorless city does not have.

The oracle arm exposed a trap worth reporting. When the target was standardised with all of a
city's stations and one station was then held out, the mean of the remaining stations equals
$-z_i/(n-1)$, which is perfectly anti-correlated with the held-out value; kriging appeared to
score ρ = −0.833 for this reason alone. Fitting the normalisation on training stations only
removed the artefact [F.105].

### 2.8.3 The benchmark

Every test compares a candidate against the same benchmark: the fraction of built-up land cover
within 2.4 km of each station, used as it is, with nothing fitted. It was the best of the 60
predictors, with a median within-city correlation of 0.309 on the 46-city frame with at least
eight stations per city (positive in 35 of 46 cities) and 0.301 on the 47-city frame with at least
six [learned-pattern plan; F.105]. Skill rose with buffer radius up to 2.4 km, which is coarser
than the 1 km scale of a typical satellite-derived map. The benchmark is the best predictor we
assembled, not a theoretical ceiling; a predictor we did not test could beat it.

### 2.8.4 Detection limit

A null result is only as informative as the smallest effect the experiment could have seen. We
fixed that quantity before the learned-pattern test. We simulated paired within-city differences
drawn from a normal distribution with the observed between-city spread (standard deviation 0.290,
taken from the paired difference between the benchmark and night-time lights), and found the
smallest true effect for which a one-sample Wilcoxon test detects a positive median at p < 0.05
in at least 80% of 4,000 simulated panels. With 46 cities, that minimum detectable effect is
δ = 0.130 in rank correlation; with eight cities it would have been 0.320 [learned-pattern plan].
A candidate beats the benchmark only if its paired advantage exceeds δ with an interval that
excludes δ. Where an interval includes zero but also includes gains up to δ, we report the result
as *undetectable at δ*, not as evidence that no effect exists [@Lakens2017; @Lakens2018].
A detection limit depends on the spread of the specific paired difference, so the single δ above
fits only the comparison it was simulated for. We therefore report, for every test, the limit
computed by the same simulation from that test's own paired differences: 0.130 for the learned
pattern, 0.180 and 0.080 for the two embedding tests, and 0.09 to 0.16 for the model families.
The registered bars (which used 0.130) are applied as registered; the test-specific limits state
what each test could in fact have detected.

### 2.8.5 The four tests

**Learned pattern (registered, OSF 2jyfg).** Machine-learning models (a neural network, a random
forest and ridge regression, each averaged over five seeds) were trained leave-one-city-out on the
60 predictors to learn a transferable within-city pattern. The registered bar was an absolute
median correlation of at least 0.44, the benchmark plus δ.
[Deviation to disclose: the registration fixed the neural network as the lead learner and listed
meteorological drivers, time and terrain among the inputs; the analysis compared the best of the
three learners by median and used static predictors only. Neither change can have favoured the
null: choosing the best learner can only raise the candidate's score.]

**Earth-observation foundation-model embeddings (registered, OSF 6udm3).** The 64-dimensional
annual embeddings of the AlphaEarth Foundations model [@Brown2025]
for 2023 were averaged within 100 m of each station (coverage 100% of 636 stations) and used in
ridge regression, leave-one-city-out. Three confirmatory tests were registered: embeddings alone
against the benchmark (E1), embeddings added to the 60 predictors (E2), and the partial
correlation of embeddings with PM2.5 after the benchmark is removed (E3). A pass required a paired
advantage above δ with its interval excluding δ; E3 was tested against zero. The registration
also made any pass provisional on checking that the embedding model was not trained on ground
monitors, since contamination could only have inflated a score.

**Seven model families (exploratory, post hoc).** In response to an external review, we ran six
admissible families against the benchmark: ridge regression on all predictors, stepwise land-use
regression (the conventional approach, which in published campaigns reaches R² of 0.43 to 0.83
[@Hoek2008]), random forests, gradient boosting, a mixed model with a city random
intercept, and a Gaussian process on covariates, plus the oracle arm above. Each is judged
against δ = 0.130, which this analysis reuses from the learned-pattern registration [F.105].

**Deliberate against convenience siting (exploratory, post hoc).** Published land-use regression
campaigns choose sites to span land-use contrasts, whereas regulatory networks are sited for
compliance. To test whether this explains the gap between published skill and ours, we used the
43 cities with at least ten stations and made each into both designs: in 40 repeats, a model
(ridge regression on seven covariates) was fitted on half of a city's stations chosen either by a
conditioned Latin hypercube over the covariates (deliberate) or in order of proximity to major
roads (convenience), and scored on the other half. The comparison is paired within city [F.103].

**Supporting exploratory analyses.** Earlier, lower-powered checks on the same frame are reported in
Supplementary Note S3.2: single global proxies (best: population within 2 km, ρ = 0.207), fitted regime
weights, inverse-distance interpolation from a city's own network (worse than assuming the city
is uniform in 23 of 30 cities), and a full land-use regression predictor set, which moved the pooled
correlation only from 0.273 to 0.275 [F.58–F.61].

### 2.8.6 Reporting

Every result is a paired within-city difference with a bootstrap interval over cities
(4,000 draws), accompanied by the number of cities in which the candidate won. Registered
verdicts are reported as registered; exploratory analyses are labelled as such and are not
counted toward any registered tally.
