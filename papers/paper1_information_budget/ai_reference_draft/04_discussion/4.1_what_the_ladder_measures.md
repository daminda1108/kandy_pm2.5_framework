# 4 Discussion

## 4.1 What the ladder measures

A gain on the ladder is the error a stream removes from the estimate one rung below it. It is
therefore a property of three things together: the stream, the city, and the estimate the stream is
added to. The first two local stations reduced error by 21.8 % on the discovery panel and by 8.5 % on
the confirmation panel. Both numbers are correct. They differ because the sensorless estimate
underneath them differs in quality. Across learners on the discovery panel the same step ranged from
22 to 41 %, and across climate bands it ranged from 52.9 % in the deep tropics, where the sensorless
estimate was weakest, to 6.0 % in the temperate confirmation cities, where it was strongest. A first
local station is worth most where a city knows least. The registered robustness tests show the
same thing from the other side: with TabPFN's weaker bottom rung the first two stations were worth 18.6 %,
and with a richer one built from five more free streams they were still worth 8.9 % (Sections
3.1.5–3.1.6).

This is why the ladder reports gains against a declared rung rather than against a fixed baseline
such as the city's long-term mean. A city deciding what to buy does not start from nothing. It starts
from whatever the free global streams already give it, and it is the increment over that starting
point that it pays for. The declared budgets make the starting point explicit and checkable
(Section 2.4). A reader who has a better sensorless estimate than ours should expect smaller gains from
every rung above it, and a reader with a worse one larger gains.

Two further boundaries follow from the design. The target is the daily mean over a city's held-out
stations, so the ladder values observations for tracking how polluted the city is from day to day. It
says nothing about where in the city the pollution is, which is the question of the spatial learning
curve (Section 2.7) and of the bounded tests in Section 3.7. And the background rung is a series built
from the city's own network: the daily 10th percentile of the stations not used elsewhere. It measures
what a background level is worth when it is known well. A regional station outside the city recovers
only part of it. An independent network 30 to 300 km away recovered 71 % [44, 82] of the rung's gain
per city, about 83 % at around 60 km and 45 % at around 150 km [F.116]. The confirmed ranking of the
background above the first local stations therefore applies to an external regional station only as
far as that station tracks the floor of the city's own concentrations.
