## 4.2 What a city without monitors should measure first

The confirmation supports four pieces of advice for cities like those it covered, which were mostly
temperate and subtropical and mostly served by regulatory networks.

First, use the free streams before buying anything. Reanalysis drivers, a map of the city's surface
and a daily satellite retrieval can be assembled for any city at no cost. Every gain reported here is
measured on top of them, so a city that skips this step will see larger gains from its first
instruments without being better off.

Second, one or two local stations improve the ordinary-day estimate modestly and reliably: by 8.5 %
[3.1, 25.1] on the confirmation panel, with the improvement positive in 82 % of cities. They do not
improve the estimate on the days that matter most for health. On days at or above each city's 90th
percentile their effect was −2.7 [−10.3, +3.7], and they changed the classification of almost no
guideline exceedance.

Third, stations beyond the second add almost nothing to a daily city-level estimate. Stations three to
six added 0.22 points [0.12, 0.50], inside the equivalence bound registered in advance. A city that
needs more than two stations needs them for a spatial purpose (locating hotspots, siting
interventions, exposure mapping), and should judge them against that purpose. For that purpose the
spatial learning curve suggests an order of magnitude rather than a number: in most dense networks,
tropical ones included, interpolation from a handful of reference stations begins to rank neighbourhoods
better than a free land-cover layer, and each station informs about the kilometre around it
(Section 3.6.4).

Fourth, a background series is the most valuable single observation, and more valuable than the first
local stations. On the ordinary day it outranked them by 24.6 points [4.1, 47.8]. For exceedances of
the WHO 24-hour guideline it outranked them by 59.3 points [33.9, 67.7]. A city whose first question is
"on which days are we above the guideline?" should look first for a background series. In practice this
means a regional monitor or a network within about 60 km that shares its regional air, with the loss
described in Section 4.1. The exceedance ranking held under every starting estimate and learner we tried;
the ordinary-day ranking did not resolve when the sensorless estimate was weak (Section 3.1.6).

In the deep tropics the advice is open. On the discovery panel the first local stations led the
background for the ordinary day (−27.4 [−47.8, +11.8], 12 cities), but the interval included zero and
the lead shrank in the prospective arm. Even there, the background led for exceedances (+7.7 [+0.1,
+34.3]). The confirmation could not test a latitude dependence, because only four fresh low-latitude
cities with dense networks exist in the public archives. For a tropical city the two observations
should therefore be treated as complementary rather than ranked: local stations for the ordinary day,
a background series for the episode. [Author: this is the paragraph that connects to Paper 2 and to
