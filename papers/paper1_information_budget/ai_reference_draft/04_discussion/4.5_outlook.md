## 4.5 Outlook

The question this study could not answer is the one that matters most for the cities with the least
monitoring: does the value of a local station relative to a background change in the tropics? Public
archives now hold four fresh deep-tropical or tropical cities with dense networks, too few to test a
latitude effect. The test is registered in form (the moderator M1) and can be run unchanged when
more tropical networks exist. The rapid spread of low-cost sensors makes this plausible within a few
years, and a registered prospective test on those networks would be the most useful next step.

The spatial learning curve (Section 3.6) bounds what stations buy for a map in the densest networks, but its frame holds one tropical city. Repeating it on dense low-cost-sensor networks in the tropics would say whether the split between cities where interpolation helps early and cities where it never does depends on climate or on network design.

Two smaller questions are ready to test. The partial correlation of Earth-observation embeddings with
station means, after the built-up benchmark is removed, missed its registered bar by 0.007 (Section 3.7).
A larger panel of stations would settle whether that signal is real. And the value of a regional
background measured by an independent network, rather than by the city's own, should be measured
directly as a function of distance and shared meteorology. That is the quantity a city actually buys.

Finally, the ladder values observations for a daily estimate made after the fact. A city that wants a
forecast will value them differently, because a background series carries information about incoming
air that a local station cannot. Extending the ladder to forecast lead times is a direct application
of the same design.
