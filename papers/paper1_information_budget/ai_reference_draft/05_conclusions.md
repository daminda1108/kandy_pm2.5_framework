# 5 Conclusions

We measured what observations add to a daily estimate of city PM2.5 that begins from free global data.
We did this under declared, code-checked information budgets, paired within city, averaged over many
random station splits, and confirmed once on 72 cities that were registered before their data were
retrieved.

In that population, which is mostly temperate and served by regulatory monitors, the registered
ladder found that two local stations used to recalibrate the free estimate reduce daily error by about
9 %, four more add a quarter of a point, and a background series read day by day adds about 40 %. These
results held under a richer starting estimate, three other learners and full station networks. A
post-hoc re-analysis showed what they measure. When every stream is used the same way, two stations read
on the day reduce error by close to 58 %, whichever stations they are, and the background's registered
lead disappears. The value of a station to a daily city estimate lies in its daily reading; a short
calibration campaign recovers a small fraction of it, and the kind of station, beyond two, matters
little.

For a map the evidence is weaker than the registered rules suggested. At three to eight stations,
neither interpolation nor any free product, a built-up layer or a satellite PM2.5 surface, ranked
neighbourhoods usefully; once multiple testing was accounted for, interpolation clearly overtook the free
layer in 3 of 23 cities; siting by design gained nothing; and tropical cities could not be distinguished
from temperate ones at the resolution the data allow.

Whether any of this differs in the tropics, where monitoring is scarcest, remains open: the public
record holds too few dense tropical networks to test it.

The method may matter as much as the numbers, and so may its limits. Two striking exploratory results, a
four-fold advantage for local stations in the deep tropics and a monitor-trained covariate halving a
station's value, did not survive split-averaging and pairing. A registered ordering did not survive a
like-for-like comparison. Declared budgets, paired inference, averaging over random design choices and a
single registered test on fresh data protect how a comparison is run; they do not protect what it
compares. Arms built the same way, and checked to be built the same way before registration, are the
safeguard this study lacked.
